In this lecture, we explore the history of graphics processing units or GPUs, their evolution,

and their pivotal role in modern data centers. Originally designed to render computer graphics,

GPUs have grown into essential components for accelerating complex computations across a range of

industries. GPUs were initially created to rapidly process and render images for display devices.

The human reliance on vision for processing computer-generated information made efficient

image rendering essential. Early GPUs focused on managing pixels, the smallest unit of a digital

image, which determined characteristics like position, color, and brightness. As screen resolutions

advanced, GPUs evolved to handle the increasing processing demands of higher pixel densities,

laying the foundation for their role in modern computing. In the 1980s, we saw the introduction of

graphics display processes for specific visual tasks. Moving into the 1990s, the development of

modular graphics cards allowed these components to be replaced or upgraded in computer systems.

Today, GPUs are indispensable for a wide range of applications, from gaming to high performance

computing and AI. GPU architecture forms the backbone of modern graphics processing and accelerated

computing. A typical GPU is built around thousands of cores, onboard cache memory, and high-speed

GPU memory. Together, these components enable parallel processing, where multiple tasks are

executed simultaneously for maximum efficiency. Cores, a thousands of processing units designed for

parallel execution, allowing GPUs to excel at handling large data sets and complex computations.

Cache memory provides fast, reliable data access to support the cause. High-speed memory is

specifically optimized for GPU tasks, enabling data sharing between GPUs and accelerating performance.

For example, a state-of-the-art Nvidia H100 GPU includes over 10,000 cores and supports memory

bandwidth of two terabytes per second, offering unmatched computational power.

The exponential growth in AI model complexity has driven demand for GPU acceleration.

In just three years, state-of-the-art AI models have increased in size by three orders of magnitude.

CPUs alone cannot meet these demands, but GPUs, with their specialized parallel processing capabilities,

provide a clear path forward. Nvidia has positioned itself as a leader in the AI stack

Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of CPUs and GPUs is critical to appreciating

their complementary roles in computing. CPUs are optimized for sequential processing and low latency

tasks, with fast clock speeds and large memory caches. They excel at general-purpose applications,

but struggle with compute-intensive parallel workloads. On the other hand, GPUs are designed

for parallel processing, with thousands of cores capable of executing many simple tasks simultaneously.

GPUs are ideal for tasks such as AI training, rendering, and high performance simulations.

Key differences performance per watt. GPUs dedicate most of their silicon to computation,

offering higher efficiency compared to CPUs. Memory bandwidth. GPUs feature significantly faster memory,

enabling improved performance for bandwidth bound applications.

From their origins, in graphics rendering to their modern role in AI and high performance computing,
